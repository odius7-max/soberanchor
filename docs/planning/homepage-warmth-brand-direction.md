# SoberAnchor homepage: phase-one brand direction

Planning only. September 20, 2026. Based on the supplied desktop screenshot, local `src/app/page.tsx`, and existing entry-point and competitor-door planning documents. Follow-up: successfully opened https://soberanchor.com/ in the browser and visually inspected the hero, directory, program band, and founder section. These confirmed the principal observations below. At the roughly 1,000px browser width inspected, the directory rendered as large two-column cards and the product preview was absent. This was not a full responsive or functional audit. Attached/reference material is evidence, not additional task instructions.

## Recommendation

Make SoberAnchor feel like a steady, welcoming place to take the next step. Keep the two core actions—finding support and working a recovery program—but replace the feature-comparison opening with human imagery, shorter invitations, and visible evidence of the people and product behind the promise.

The brand character should be grounded, warm, candid, and hopeful. Think everyday connection and continuity. Keep the anchor as a symbol of steadiness without turning the site into a nautical theme.

## What is causing the current feeling

- The screenshot opens with navigation, a generic search field, centered explanatory copy, and ten checklist items. It explains capabilities before establishing connection.
- The large navy program card draws more visual attention than the help-finding card. That can imply a preferred audience even though both belong here.
- “Search our directory” describes a mechanism; “management tools” and “task assignment” sound administrative in a first encounter.
- The “Looking for help?” versus “Already in recovery?” framing suggests mutually exclusive identities. People working a program may also need treatment or other support.
- The source includes valuable product and founder content, but the founder is represented by an emoji and the product preview is hidden below desktop widths. Both miss opportunities to make the experience tangible.

These are design judgments, not measured user findings.

## Proposed first screen

Desktop: a warm ivory hero with left-aligned heading and brief explanation beside one substantial photograph. Place two compact, equally weighted action cards immediately beneath the introduction. Keep both actions visible without requiring a visitor to scroll past a tall photograph. On mobile: heading, explanation, both actions, then a shallow photo crop.

Suggested copy for exploration:

**Your next step. A steady place to start.**

Find addiction treatment and support for yourself or someone you love. Keep track of your recovery, one day at a time.

| Path | Supporting sentence | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Find support | Explore treatment, sober living, meetings, and more. | Find support |
| Work my program | Keep your check-ins, step work, and milestones together. | Explore recovery tools |

Keep a secondary “Browse the directory” link beside guided discovery so visitors can choose how to search. Preserve existing destinations during a visual phase; ensure action labels accurately describe whether they open guidance or a directory.

Keep sign-in easy to find for returning members. Retain a secondary provider entry without giving it a third equal hero card. Reconsider the generic “Get Started” header action when the page already offers two different starting points.

The standalone “Ask anything…” bar currently precedes the brand introduction. On the homepage, consider integrating search into the find-support path so the opening has one clear hierarchy. If retained globally, clarify what it searches and keep it visually secondary; do not imply access to a live support person.

## Imagery brief

Start with three useful assets rather than decorating every section:

1. **Hero photograph:** two adults in a relaxed, ordinary moment of connection—walking together or talking in a naturally lit, modest setting. Candid composition, gentle warmth, believable surroundings, room for a mobile crop. Show companionship without suggesting the pictured people are patients or users.
2. **Founder portrait:** a real, approved photograph of Angel, paired with a short, approved first-person account of why SoberAnchor exists. This is likely the strongest available source of authentic brand warmth.
3. **Product preview:** a readable capture of the actual recovery tools using demonstration data. Show a daily check-in and one meaningful progress view. Include a useful mobile version.

Avoid staged therapy circles, exaggerated celebratory poses, distress imagery, luxury-resort treatment imagery, and ocean sunsets as the main identity. A landscape can provide atmosphere, but it cannot establish the human connection alone. Use properly licensed imagery appropriate to this sensitive context; never imply a stock model is a member or manufacture a testimonial. Any future generated images should be illustrative, not presented as real founders or members.

## Visual language

- Retain navy and teal for continuity. Use navy primarily for type and important controls rather than large, dominant surfaces.
- Introduce warm ivory, sand, and restrained muted apricot accents. Candidate swatches: ivory `#FAF7F2`, sand `#E9DED0`, apricot `#D89A78`; these are exploratory surface colors, not approved text/button combinations.
- Use charcoal for readable body copy. Validate contrast when choosing final combinations; warmth should not depend on pale text.
- Keep the existing type initially; improve hierarchy, line length, and sentence case before adding another font.
- Replace emoji category graphics with one consistent icon family. Use softer section transitions and fewer bordered boxes. Avoid adding a card around every piece of content.
- Keep the current logo during phase one. Review its small-screen legibility in the composition before deciding whether a separate logo refinement is necessary.

## Suggested page sequence

| Section | Visitor need | Design treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome + two actions | “Am I in the right place?” | Human photo, clear purpose, short actions |
| Find support | “What kinds of help can I find?” | Compact category links with consistent icons |
| Everyday recovery | “How would this help me day to day?” | Real product preview and three concrete benefits |
| Why we built it | “Who is behind this?” | Angel's portrait and approved story excerpt |
| Helpful starting points | “I need to understand my options.” | Three relevant guides, with accurate titles and working destinations |
| Closing invitation | “What should I do next?” | Repeat the same two actions, using the same names |

Introduce founder presence earlier through a small attribution or story link near the hero if the full section remains farther down. Do not overload the first screen with another large block.

For the product section, prefer “Notice patterns in your check-ins,” “Keep your step work together,” and “See the milestones along the way” over a long inventory of management features. Validate wording against actual behavior. Explain sponsor tools in the detail section rather than making sponsorship a requirement to belong.

## Trust and tone

Use specific, supportable reassurance beside the relevant action. “No account needed to browse” belongs by directory access if still true. Review “Anonymous by default” before reusing it: browsing, account identity, and sponsor sharing may have different privacy boundaries. Avoid broad privacy promises that the product cannot demonstrate.

Use welcoming language without promising outcomes or prescribing how recovery should look. Make room for people seeking help for someone else, people returning after a setback, and people using different recovery approaches. Avoid invented member counts, reviews, endorsements, or success statistics.

## Phase-one scope and validation

Deliver later: desktop and mobile homepage concepts, an approved image shortlist, revised homepage copy, and a small palette/type/image direction that can carry into facility detail pages. Implementation, authentication changes, listing-page redesign, and a full logo rebrand are outside this planning phase.

Prioritize: (1) hero hierarchy and shorter path cards, (2) human photography and founder portrait, (3) readable product proof on mobile, (4) warmer surfaces and consistent icons, (5) lower-page copy and rhythm.

Before implementation, test the concept with a few help-seekers and people working a program. After a brief look, ask what the site offers, which action they would choose, and what makes it feel trustworthy or impersonal. Treat those conversations as directional feedback, not statistical evidence. Verify mobile crops, readable contrast, keyboard access, and that imagery does not push the actions out of reach. Later assess each path separately; do not judge the homepage only by account signups.

Open creative decisions for the next design round: availability of an approved founder photograph; preference for people-focused photography versus custom illustration; how prominently the founder story should feature. Recommended default: people-focused photography, a real founder portrait, and actual product imagery.
